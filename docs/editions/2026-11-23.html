Signals

GovernanceProduction now

Credentials now leak through what coding agents write and what AI providers keep, and canary tokens are how teams find out.

Together AI disclosed on Nov 21 that an AI coding agent working on its public xorl repository committed a credential (TechBooky, explainx). It opened a private Hugging Face repository of customer prompt samples. Scanning caught it, but an outsider had already downloaded the samples, searched them for other credentials and tested some. Together now uses short-lived credentials, checks every pull request for secrets and has tightened its coding-agent rules.

Five of the credentials the intruder tested were canaries from a Dragos study published Nov 19 (Dragos, Dataconomy). Dragos sent about 2 million prompts carrying unique URLs and fake AWS keys to AI services since July and watched where they surfaced. Data from at least three providers was used against its test systems, in two cases from requests marked for zero data retention.

On Nov 22 OpenRouter dropped three providers, removed 125 endpoints from its zero data retention list and said it will run Dragos-style tests continuously (Notebookcheck). No provider has been named.

Why it matters: Prompts and agent commits are now credential stores. Keep long-lived keys out of agent sessions, scan agent commits before push, and use canary credentials to test what providers keep.

"A retention promise from an AI provider is a claim until you test it. Keep real keys out of prompts and plant canary credentials to see where your data goes."
Dragos canary study, Nov 19, 2026; Together AI disclosure, Nov 21; OpenRouter, Nov 22

GovernanceExperiment now

Agent credentials and approvals are moving into gateways outside the agent, and those gateways are now a target.

LangSmith Tool Gateway entered public beta on Nov 19 (Automater, daily.dev). It holds the credentials, runs tool calls as the user or the agent, can require human approval for sensitive calls and traces each one. Databricks' Genie ZeroOps, in beta the same day, drafts fixes for failing jobs but changes nothing in production without human approval (Databricks, docs). Meanwhile Amazon reports attacker agents, likely built on the ARTEX framework, pulling stored keys from exposed LiteLLM and Dify instances (AWS Security blog, VentureBeat). Amazon could not verify what they achieved.

Why it matters: Moving keys out of the agent is right, but it concentrates them. Keep the gateway off the internet and test its approvals like any other control.

"When AI agents call tools through a gateway, that gateway holds every key they use. Keep it off the internet and test its approvals like a production control."
LangSmith Tool Gateway and Databricks Genie ZeroOps betas, Nov 19, 2026; Amazon Threat Intelligence on ARTEX, Nov 2026

Engineering flowWatch closely

Agent swarms now ship with a hard spending cap and a separate judge as standard parts.

Replit's Missions, launched Nov 17 and covered through the weekend, runs a swarm of agents toward a goal with user-set success criteria, a budget and actions that need sign-off (The New Stack, RuntimeWire). Spending is checked at every step and stops at the cap, goal met or not. A separate AI judge checks the results. Replit says it built an internal monitoring platform in two days for $3,000. The figures are Replit's.

Why it matters: In a swarm, cost and "done" are what drift. Ask any vendor for both set by a person before the run and enforced outside the agents.

"An agent swarm is only as safe as its budget cap and its definition of done. Set both before the run starts, and enforce them outside the agents."
Replit Missions launch, via The New Stack, Nov 2026
